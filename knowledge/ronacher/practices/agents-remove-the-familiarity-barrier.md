# Agents remove familiarity as a barrier to language and domain choice

Agents decouple technology choice from personal fluency: you no longer need to
already know a language or a hard domain to work in it, because the agent
carries the unfamiliarity cost that used to gate the decision. That flips who
picks what and why — language and domain choice increasingly track what people
*want* (speed, marketing, aesthetics) rather than what they already *know*.

Ronacher's read on the "hard languages" trend: a wave of engineers now ship
Rust who previously wouldn't have touched it, and even Zig — despite parts of
its community being openly anti-AI — is picking up projects like Cloudflare's
Git-protocol engine and Vercel's `fx` coding agent, both LLM-assisted. The same
pattern shows up one level up, in domains rather than languages: people are now
doing serious work with DWARF, eBPF, custom network drivers, and custom crypto
— areas that were previously off-limits to most developers, in crypto's case
partly because the practitioners *deliberately* gatekept it. The unlock isn't
that any of this got objectively easier; it's that not personally knowing it
stopped being disqualifying.

This complements rather than contradicts
[[pick-an-agent-legible-language]]: that concept is about which language the
*agent* performs best in (well-represented weights, good tooling, low churn —
Go over Rust or Python). This one is about which language the *human* is
willing to attempt — and the two forces can point in opposite directions,
which is exactly why marketing and vibes now carry real weight in language
choice: the human no longer needs a legibility reason to pick something. Domain
knowledge still measurably helps output quality — "you don't necessarily need
to know all the tricks... though knowledge greatly helps" — but it's no longer
the gate it used to be.
