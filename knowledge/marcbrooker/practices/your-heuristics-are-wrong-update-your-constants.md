# Your heuristics are wrong — get back on the ice and update your constants

Brooker's career/methodology advice for the agentic shift: the rules of thumb
experienced engineers built over their careers are an "extinction-level event"
for heuristics, and the only way to recalibrate is to build with the new tools
firsthand. Aimed at senior ICs, juniors, and the field as a whole.

## The costs that went to zero

The framing premise (*You are here*): the cost of turning written business logic
into code has dropped to ~zero; the cost of integration/plumbing has dropped to
~zero; the cost of building efficient, reliable, secure *end-to-end systems* is
dropping, but slowly. By Amdahl's Law, as the easy part speeds up it stops being
the bottleneck, so the remaining value concentrates in the hard part — building
systems — and in **ideas** (integration and translation are solved; transformative
new ideas remain scarce and, as the lever lengthens, more valuable). "Software's
first act is over." There's a real road of loss (programming as a beloved,
historically over-valued craft, ending like weaving or coopering — the joy
remains, but pursue it intentionally) and a road of opportunity; denial is the one
irrational response. "You are here."

## For the senior IC: humility, courage, curiosity

Many hard-won heuristics are now wrong — what "maintainable" means, the cost of
writing code vs. integrating libraries vs. taking dependencies, what a good API
is, where service boundaries go, where security/data-integrity is enforced, what's
easy and what's hard. Not *all* of them: taste, high standards, and deep
business/customer/technical understanding are *more* valuable than ever. The job
of the tech leader becomes "the humility to accept that many of your heuristics
are wrong, the courage to believe some are still right, and the curiosity to
actively learn the difference." The Cool Runnings trap: you can't coach from the
sidelines remembering how you *used* to bobsled — "we're not bobsledding anymore."

The recalibration method is concrete and empirical: **get back on the ice.**
Build, own, get your hands dirty, ship a prototype, run a thousand little
experiments in an afternoon, attempt something you'd have assumed impossible or
unaffordable, then examine what you learned and **update your constants**. The
least valuable colleague over the next couple of years is the experienced person
unwilling to change their thinking; the most valuable is the one combining
curiosity with experience.

## For the junior: engage earlier with the essence of engineering

The long, insulated apprenticeship spent *learning the craft* is becoming less
valuable as automation takes over the craft. The new junior path engages much
earlier with the **essence of engineering** — "deeply understanding the problem,
the constraints, the tools, and the environment, and coming up with an optimal
solution" (Wellington: "do for a tickey what any fool can do for a dollar"). More
feet-on-the-ground (economics, product, people) *and* more head-in-the-clouds
(the deep science and theory that "is barely changing at all" and gives the
engineer a rock to stand on). Day to day, the advice is unchanged from his 2019
distributed-systems guidance: own something real, be a builder, **carry a pager,
own a deadline — "reality cannot be fooled."** Juniors hold one structural
advantage in a time of change: they're already beginners and know they must
learn, while the hard thing for seniors is going back to being a beginner.
