# Model-weight-first companies

Geoff Huntley introduces a new category of companies that build "with the grain" of large language models rather than fighting against their inherent preferences. These model-weight-first companies optimize for AI development by aligning with model training data rather than imposing pre-existing corporate standards.

## Core concept

"From here on out I'm going to call to these companies as model weight first. This category of companies can be defined as any company that is building with the data ('grain') that has been baked into the large language models."

Model-weight-first companies embrace the preferences encoded in LLM training data—naming conventions, architectural patterns, and implementation approaches that models naturally generate—rather than forcing models to conform to legacy corporate standards.

## Working with the grain metaphor

Drawing from woodworking, Huntley emphasizes working with rather than against natural patterns: "In woodworking, there's a saying that you should work with the grain, not against the grain."

When code is generated using model preferences and then re-read into context windows, "it is consuming its preferred choice of tokens"—creating a virtuous cycle of model-friendly development.

## Competitive advantages

Model-weight-first companies achieve superior AI outcomes because they:

- **Minimize context engineering overhead**: No need to stuff context windows with rules overriding model preferences
- **Maximize usable context window**: More space available for actual requirements rather than corporate policy enforcement
- **Reduce AI resistance**: Models work more naturally when not forced against their training
- **Accelerate development velocity**: Less friction in agent workflows

"The less that you allocate (i.e., cursor rules or what else have you), then you'll have more context window available for actually implementing requirements of the software that needs to be built."

## Contrast with traditional corporates

Traditional corporate approaches create friction by:

- Enforcing arbitrary coding standards (snake_case vs PascalCase vs kebab-case)
- Requiring complex infrastructure setup that models struggle with
- Imposing legacy architectural decisions that conflict with model training
- Spending context window budget on policy enforcement rather than actual work

"At a model weight first company, building a docker image is easy but at a corporate the agent will have one hell of a time and end up with a suboptimal/disappointing outcome."

## Transformation imperative

"Perhaps the corporates who succeed the most with the adoption of AI will be those who shed their dogma that no longer applies and start leaning into transforming to become model-weight-first companies."

Success with AI adoption may depend more on organizational willingness to adapt to model preferences than on forcing models to adapt to existing corporate structures.

## Fundamental shift in software conventions

Huntley questions whether human-centric coding conventions still matter: "Such policies were created for humans to create consistency so humans can comprehend the codebase. Something that is of a lesser concern now that AI is here."

This represents a fundamental shift from optimizing code for human readability to optimizing for AI development efficiency.
