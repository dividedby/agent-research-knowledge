# Technical communication is a durable edge because it isn't a trainable, verifiable skill

Models keep getting better at coding while getting worse at writing, and
that's not a coincidence likely to self-correct — good writing resists the
same optimization loop that made coding and math better. You can generate
coding or math problems and grade them automatically; you can't auto-grade
good writing. The available substitute, human RLHF raters, rewards prose that
sounds impressive skimmed one paragraph at a time — the actual origin of the
"stick three hundred writing devices into every sentence" style that reads as
AI slop. Hand-picking graders with good taste is possible in principle but
doesn't scale to the training-data volumes labs need, so it isn't happening.

A second, compounding cause: labs are optimizing for capability, not
communication, and the trade-off shows up directly in the prose. Model
internal reasoning tokens read as compressed, near-telegraphic shorthand
optimized purely for problem-solving; the "Claudish" style readers notice —
semi-baroque, semi-truncated, nobody enjoys it — reads like a literal
translation of that compressed internal reasoning into English, not a
stylistic choice. Translating it back into genuinely good prose needs both
technical understanding of the underlying problem and the ability to write
well, which is exactly the compound skill a model (or an unskilled human)
struggles to substitute for.

Communicating to humans carries a second, independent advantage: readers are
developing an "AI-blind" reflex — the same skip-past-it instinct people use on
flashing ad banners — that fires on AI-typical prose regardless of whether the
underlying content is good. Circulate a strategy doc that reads as
AI-written and most colleagues will force themselves through it word by word
rather than actually engage with it; the identical content, human-authored,
gets read normally. That reflex is what makes human-authored technical
writing more valuable precisely as AI-authored writing becomes more common,
not less — it's a skill that gets scarcer in relative terms even while every
individual writer's raw output looks replaceable.
