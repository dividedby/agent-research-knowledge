# Agent-speed work crowds out slow thinking — protect it deliberately

Working at the throughput coding agents enable pulls your day toward a single
cognitive mode — rapidly skimming an agent's output and judging it ("is this
schema change right? do these numbers look plausible?") — and away from the slow,
reflective thinking that produces a real theory of a system. That skimming-and-
judging mode is a genuine skill, not mindless work, but it isn't a substitute for
depth, and the fix isn't to simply slow down: when your tools let you solve a task
ten times faster, competitive pressure makes not using that speed costly.

**The throughput lever is running several role-differentiated agent sessions on
one task in parallel, not one session per task.** A single task might run six or
seven sessions at once — one for exploration, two or three for competing
implementation attempts, two or three for review, one for manual testing —
switching attention between them as each produces output. This is how the
frenetic mode compounds: it isn't context-switching between tasks, it's
context-switching between many simultaneous angles on the same task.

**Why the frenetic mode specifically erodes thinking, not just time:** composing
prose in your own words is what forces a vague, directional sense of an idea to
become an actual idea — you don't really have an idea until you've had to
articulate it. This mechanism does not transfer to writing *with* an LLM, even
iteratively, because the LLM is doing the articulating. If agent-throughput work
displaces the time you'd otherwise spend writing by hand, you lose the mechanism
that builds theories, not just spare hours. The corresponding fix is specific and
outside the loop: deliberately keep writing in your own words, and read
dense, slow, information-heavy material (non-fiction books hold up especially
well) rather than only consuming agent output.

**This isn't optional forever, either.** There are still ordinary engineering
problems current agents can produce working answers to but not *tasteful* ones —
Goedecke's example is a large refactor across a complicated codebase — so the
slow-thinking capacity still gets called on at work, and losing the habit
entirely would be a mistake even under throughput pressure.
